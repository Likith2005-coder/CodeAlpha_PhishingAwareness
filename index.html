Phishing awareness training

It looks fine.
That is the entire point.

A phishing email is not supposed to look suspicious — it is designed to look like every other message in your inbox. Below is a real-world style credential phish. Read it the way you would read it at 9am on a Monday. Then mark it up.

Exhibit A Microsoft 365 credential phish
Inbox — 1 unread
From
Microsoft 365 Security Team <>
To
Subject

Our system has detected unusual sign-in activity on your account and your mailbox storage has exceeded the permitted limit. Your account is scheduled for deactivation.

To keep your mailbox active, please using the secure link below.

Verify My Account

Failure to verify within 24 hours will result in permanent loss of all emails, contacts and files.

Microsoft Account Team

↩ Reply ↪ Forward 🗑 Delete

Examiner's notes

0 of 6 marked

  1. The sender domain is not Microsoft

    Read it closely: rnicrosoft — an r followed by an n, imitating an m. At small sizes the two are almost identical. The real domain would end in @microsoft.com.

  2. You are not the only recipient

    undisclosed-recipients means this went out in bulk. A genuine security alert about your account is addressed to you alone.

  3. A deadline you did not agree to

    “Within 24 hours” exists to stop you thinking. Urgency is the single most reliable marker of a phish, because deliberation is the attacker's only real enemy.

  4. It does not know your name

    Your provider knows who you are. “Dear User”, “Dear Customer” or “Dear Account Holder” means the sender is working from a list, not from your account.

  5. No one legitimate asks for your password

    This is the bright line. Microsoft, your bank and your college already have what they need to verify you. A request to “confirm your password” is the payload of the attack, not a security step.

  6. The link goes somewhere else entirely

    Read a domain right to left from the final slash. The registrable domain here is session-verify.ru — microsoft-account.com is just a subdomain an attacker typed in to reassure you. See the dissector below.

The six signals

What you are actually checking

You do not need to inspect mail headers or read source code. Six questions catch the overwhelming majority of phishing attempts, and all six can be asked in about ten seconds.

  • Who really sent this?

    Check the address, not the display name. Anyone can set a display name to “PayPal”. The domain after the @ is the part that cannot be faked without owning it.

  • Where does the link really go?

    Hover it — or long-press on mobile — and read the real destination. The visible text is just text, and can say anything at all.

  • Was I expecting this?

    An unexpected invoice, delivery notice, or password alert is worth one moment of suspicion, however ordinary it looks.

  • Is it rushing me?

    Deadlines, threats of closure, “final notice”. Pressure is engineered to prevent the checks you are running right now.

  • Is it asking for something it should not need?

    Passwords, OTP codes, card numbers, remote access. No legitimate organisation asks for these by email.

  • Does the tone fit the sender?

    Odd grammar, a greeting that does not know your name, or a manager who suddenly writes very differently — and only over email.

Fake websites

Read the address right to left

Almost every fake-website trick is the same trick: put a name you trust somewhere in the address where it has no authority. The only part that identifies a site is the registrable domain — the label directly before the ending, plus the ending itself. Everything to its left is chosen freely by whoever owns it.

The padlock does not mean the site is safe

It means traffic to the site is encrypted — nothing more. Certificates are free and issued in minutes, so a phishing page can and usually does have one. A padlock tells you nobody is reading your data in transit. It says nothing about who is waiting for it at the other end.

Social engineering

The levers being pulled on you

Phishing is not a technical attack. It is a persuasion attack delivered over a technical channel — which is why it works on careful people, and why spotting the technique matters more than spotting the typo.

Authority

Impersonating someone you are not inclined to question.

Sounds like “This is the CEO. I need this handled before the board call.”

Urgency

Removing the time in which you would normally think.

Sounds like “Your account will be suspended within 24 hours.”

Fear

Threatening loss, exposure or penalty.

Sounds like “Unusual login detected. Unauthorised charges will be processed.”

Curiosity

Offering something you would like to look at.

Sounds like “Salary revision list — final.xlsx”

Familiarity

Arriving as a brand, colleague or service you already use.

Sounds like “Your Netflix payment failed — update your details.”

Reciprocity

Doing you a small favour first, so refusing feels rude.

Sounds like “I have already approved your leave — just confirm your details.”

The same attack, other channels

Spear phishing
Targeted at you specifically, using details from your employer, LinkedIn or a previous breach.
Whaling
Spear phishing aimed at executives, where a single success authorises a payment.
Smishing
Delivered by SMS — parcel redelivery, KYC updates, fake bank alerts.
Vishing
Delivered by phone call, often to talk you through “verifying” an OTP.
Quishing
A QR code in place of a link, because a printed square hides its destination completely.
BEC
Business email compromise — a real, compromised account asks for a real, routine payment.

Documented incidents

It works on organisations with security teams

Each of these is a matter of public record — court filings, regulatory disclosures or company statements. None of these victims were careless amateurs.

  1. ≈ $100M

    Google & Facebook

    2013–2015 · invoice fraud

    A Lithuanian national registered a company using the name of Quanta Computer, a genuine hardware supplier to both firms, then sent invoices for goods that were never supplied. Both companies paid. He pleaded guilty to wire fraud in 2019 and was sentenced to five years; the majority of the money was recovered.

  2. $46.7M

    Ubiquiti Networks

    2015 · business email compromise

    Attackers impersonated company executives and an outside legal representative in emails to the finance department, requesting transfers to overseas accounts. The company disclosed the loss in an SEC filing. No systems were breached — the entire attack was email and persuasion.

  3. 130 accounts

    Twitter

    July 2020 · phone spear phishing

    Attackers called employees while posing as internal IT support and talked them into entering credentials on a convincing lookalike of Twitter's own VPN login page. That access reached internal admin tooling, and accounts including Obama, Musk and Apple were used to post a Bitcoin scam.

  4. 40M cards

    Target

    2013 · supplier as the way in

    The intrusion began with a phishing email to Fazio Mechanical, a refrigeration contractor with access to Target's vendor portal. Stolen vendor credentials led to the point-of-sale network and roughly 40 million payment cards. Your weakest link may not be inside your organisation.

Playbook

Habits, and what to do at 2am

Build these habits

  • Turn on multi-factor authentication. It is the single highest-value thing on this page. A stolen password alone stops being enough.
  • Prefer an app or hardware key over SMS codes. SIM swapping and real-time relay attacks defeat text messages.
  • Navigate, don't click. Type the address yourself or use your own bookmark. It costs five seconds and defeats the entire category.
  • Verify money and credentials on a second channel. Call the person on a number you already had — never the one in the message.
  • Use a password manager. It refuses to autofill on a lookalike domain, so it notices the fake site before you do.
  • Slow down when a message makes you feel something. Urgency, fear and excitement are the attack working.

If you already clicked

  1. Do not wait, and do not hide it. Minutes matter far more than embarrassment does. Every hour of silence is an hour of access.
  2. Disconnect the device from the network if you downloaded or ran anything.
  3. Change the password from a different, trusted device — and change it anywhere you reused it.
  4. Sign out all active sessions in your account's security settings, then check for added recovery emails, forwarding rules and app passwords.
  5. Report it to your IT or security team, and to the impersonated organisation.
  6. If money moved, contact your bank immediately and ask about recall. In India, report to cybercrime.gov.in or the 1930 helpline — the first few hours are when funds can still be frozen.

Reporting a phish you fell for is not an admission of incompetence. It is the control that works after every other control has failed — and the organisations that recover fastest are the ones where saying so is easy.
